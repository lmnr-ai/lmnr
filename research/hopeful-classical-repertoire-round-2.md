# Research: Hopeful classical repertoire for Hopeful Song Studio (round 2)

## Summary

The strongest direction is not another pedagogical sonatina or miniature with a nursery-song profile, but a short, internally cadencing phrase from the nineteenth-century **song-without-words / lyrical-character-piece** tradition. My provisional top tier is Mendelssohn **Op. 30 No. 1**, Fanny Hensel **Op. 6 No. 1**, Tchaikovsky **Op. 39 No. 21 (“Sweet Dream”)**, Field **Nocturne No. 1**, Grieg **Op. 38 No. 3 (“Melody”)**, and Gade **Aquarelles Op. 19 No. 1 (“Elegie”)**; Chaminade’s **Idylle, Op. 126 No. 1** is the best later, brighter alternate.

**Material limitation:** this runtime had no web/search/browser/shell tool. At the supervisor’s direction, this is a repertoire-first brief based on established catalog knowledge and known high-trust score repositories. I could not truthfully claim page-by-page score inspection or live URL validation. Accordingly, every URL, bar number, key/meter/tempo detail, harmonic endpoint, and edition-license statement below is marked **provisional / verify in score**. The shortlist is intended to make that verification pass small and efficient, not to substitute for it.

## Research angles

1. **Musical fit:** lyrical, lightly mobile, emotionally affirmative, and non-theatrical.
2. **Excerpt engineering:** 8–20 bars that begin cleanly, survive recomposition, and end with a real cadence rather than a cut-off.
3. **Recognition and product-video risk:** avoid melodies that announce their title/composer before the product appears.
4. **Rights and source practicality:** separate public-domain composition from the copyright/license of a particular scan, engraving, MIDI, or transcription.

## Findings

### 1. Best overall source family: nineteenth-century “songs without words”

This idiom already solves much of the brief: a cantabile upper line, supportive broken-chord motion, modest dynamic range, and phrase-scale cadences. It transfers naturally to piano, bell-like UI pings, warm strings, or a hybrid without sounding like a piano exercise. Mendelssohn and Hensel offer the best balance of elegance and anonymity. [Mendelssohn IMSLP work page — URL unverified](https://imslp.org/wiki/Lieder_ohne_Worte%2C_Op.30_(Mendelssohn%2C_Felix)) · [Hensel IMSLP work page — URL unverified](https://imslp.org/wiki/4_Lieder_for_Piano%2C_Op.6_(Hensel%2C_Fanny))

### 2. “Album for the young” is not itself disqualifying

Tchaikovsky’s “Sweet Dream” and some Grieg/Gurlitt miniatures are pedagogically accessible but harmonically and texturally far above the Clementi/early-Schumann material being replaced. The useful distinction is **lyrical miniature versus didactic tune**, not adult collection versus children’s collection. [Tchaikovsky IMSLP work page — URL unverified](https://imslp.org/wiki/Children%27s_Album%2C_Op.39_(Tchaikovsky%2C_Pyotr))

### 3. Composition status and file status are separate decisions

All composers below died before 1955; the underlying compositions and their first editions are generally public domain in the United States and life-plus-70 jurisdictions, subject to confirming publication facts for the specific work. That does **not** make every modern typeset, performance, MIDI, or MusicXML public domain. IMSLP’s work pages are useful for historical scans but each file has its own regional/copyright notice; Mutopia files normally carry an explicit Creative Commons license, but an exact piece/file must be found and its license checked. A user-uploaded MuseScore transcription must not be assumed reusable merely because the composition is old. [IMSLP copyright policy — URL unverified](https://imslp.org/wiki/IMSLP:Copyright_Made_Simple) · [Mutopia licensing — URL unverified](https://www.mutopiaproject.org/legal.html)

### 4. Machine-readable material should be treated as a convenience, not the authority

No direct LilyPond/MusicXML/MIDI file is asserted in this report because I could not validate one. The safe workflow is: use a public-domain first-edition or demonstrably public-domain historical engraving as the pitch/rhythm authority; then either (a) use a clearly licensed Mutopia LilyPond file after checking its header/license and comparing it to the scan, or (b) manually transcribe only the chosen 8–20 bars. This avoids inheriting transcription errors or an incompatible modern-arrangement license.

## Ranked final set and provisional excerpt plan

The following ranks optimize **music first**, then low recognition, cadence usability, and arrangement flexibility. Bar references are **editorial-number dependent and unverified**. Do not transcribe until checked against one identified public-domain edition.

### 1. Mendelssohn — Op. 30 No. 1

- **Provisional excerpt:** **mm. 1–16** (16 bars; verify whether the edition counts any upbeat).
- **Opening function:** tonic-prolongational opening in E-flat major; accompaniment establishes pulse beneath a complete vocal-style thought.
- **Closing function:** expected return to a tonic authentic cadence at the end of the opening period; verify whether m. 16 is a PAC or a weaker I arrival leading onward.
- **Pickup:** likely no independent pickup bar; retain any melodic anacrusis inside m. 1 rather than adding silence (verify).
- **Cadence quality:** likely satisfying enough for a clean video button/reveal; if m. 16 is only half-close, extend to the next tonic cadence, capped at 20 bars.
- **Why #1:** the best combination of adult elegance, motion, transparent orchestration, and manageable recognition.

### 2. Fanny Hensel — Op. 6 No. 1

- **Provisional excerpt:** **mm. 1–16**.
- **Opening function:** tonic and tonic prolongation in G-flat major (key must be checked).
- **Closing function:** likely authentic tonic close of the first large phrase/period; chromatic voice-leading may soften the cadence.
- **Pickup:** verify; if present, include it as **m. 0** and preserve its lead-in rather than quantizing onto beat 1.
- **Cadence quality:** expected satisfying, but this is the highest-priority score check because editions may phrase/bar the opening differently.
- **Why #2:** fresher than Felix Mendelssohn, with a similar production-friendly grammar and almost no mass recognition burden.

### 3. Tchaikovsky — Op. 39 No. 21, “Sweet Dream”

- **Provisional excerpt:** **mm. 1–16**.
- **Opening function:** tonic-centered presentation phrase.
- **Closing function:** balanced consequent returning to tonic, probably with a clear authentic cadence; verify exact inversion and whether m. 16 elides into the next phrase.
- **Pickup:** likely none; verify printed opening.
- **Cadence quality:** likely the safest of the list for a self-contained 16-bar export.
- **Why #3:** immediate warmth and memorability without being a famous tune; can be modernized through timbre rather than reharmonization.

### 4. Field — Nocturne No. 1 in E-flat major, H.24

- **Provisional excerpt:** **mm. 1–12** if the first 12 form a closed unit; otherwise **mm. 1–16**.
- **Opening function:** tonic established through broken-chord accompaniment and cantabile entry.
- **Closing function:** expected tonic cadence after an antecedent/dominant detour; inspect ornaments and phrase extension carefully.
- **Pickup:** melody may enter with an upbeat over the accompaniment; preserve it, but an arrangement can begin with one accompaniment pulse before the pickup.
- **Cadence quality:** probably satisfying only after the full opening period, not at the first 8-bar punctuation.
- **Why #4:** uniquely airy and luxurious; best if the video wants calm forward drift rather than overt cheer.

### 5. Grieg — Op. 38 No. 3, “Melody”

- **Provisional excerpt:** **mm. 1–16**.
- **Opening function:** tonic song phrase with characteristic color tones/non-chordal inflection.
- **Closing function:** expected tonic return at period end; verify whether a repeated section begins immediately and whether first/second endings affect the cadence.
- **Pickup:** verify; preserve any short anacrusis.
- **Cadence quality:** expected adequate; select second-ending cadence if a repeat structure gives a firmer close.
- **Why #5:** accessible but not simplistic, and slightly earthier than the Mendelssohn/Hensel options.

### 6. Gade — Aquarelles, Op. 19 No. 1, “Elegie”

- **Provisional excerpt:** do **not** default to the first phrase; inspect for a **12–16-bar major-mode or tonic-resolving span**, tentatively **mm. 9–24**.
- **Opening function:** likely begins away from a fully grounded major tonic or continues a minor-mode premise; verify.
- **Closing function:** choose a span ending with an authentic cadence, preferably in the relative or parallel major if musically complete.
- **Pickup:** retain according to the chosen internal phrase; if excerpting internally, add no fabricated pickup harmony.
- **Cadence quality:** must be confirmed; reject if the attractive passage only sequences onward.
- **Why #6:** lowest recognition risk and most distinctive string potential, but less immediately “hopeful” and therefore more dependent on excerpt selection.

### 7. Chaminade — Op. 126 No. 1, “Idylle” (alternate pending verification)

- **Provisional excerpt:** **opening 16-bar period**, provisionally mm. 1–16.
- **Opening/closing function:** expected tonic presentation followed by tonic authentic cadence, but **not inspected and not asserted**.
- **Pickup:** verify from score.
- **Cadence quality:** unknown until inspection.
- **Why #7:** potentially the brightest low-recognition candidate, but catalog details and musical content need more validation than the top six.

## Explicit rejections

1. **Clementi, Sonatina in C, Op. 36 No. 1, first movement opening** — reject. It reads as lesson-book Classical syntax: square tonic/dominant gestures, little harmonic atmosphere, and an unmistakably elementary profile. It is functional but not premium or emotionally specific.
2. **Schumann, Album for the Young, Op. 68 Nos. 1 (“Melody”) and 5 (“Little Piece”)** — reject for this use. Both are intentionally compressed pedagogical miniatures; tasteful orchestration cannot fully hide their child-piece proportions.
3. **Grieg, Lyric Pieces, Op. 12 No. 1, “Arietta”** — reject as the default despite genuine beauty. It is short, widely anthologized, and can still signal “student recital”; use Op. 38 No. 3 instead.
4. **Mendelssohn, Op. 62 No. 6, “Spring Song”** — reject as overfamiliar and semantically on-the-nose. Any arrangement risks sounding like quotation or parody.
5. **Tchaikovsky, The Seasons, Op. 37a No. 6, “June: Barcarolle”** — reject despite excellent mood. Recognition is relatively high and its melancholy is stronger than the requested hopeful understatement.
6. **Schumann, Kinderszenen, Op. 15 No. 7, “Träumerei”** — reject: extremely recognizable, slow, sentimental, and culturally pre-loaded.
7. **Brahms, Waltz in A-flat, Op. 39 No. 15** — reject: lovely but heavily anthologized and instantly familiar to many viewers.
8. **Debussy, “Clair de lune”** — reject: overfamiliar, dynamically expansive, and difficult to quote without evoking the original more than the product.
9. **Beach, “Dreaming,” Op. 15 No. 3** — keep only as a slow-cut alternate, not a primary recommendation. Its dense, suspended atmosphere may feel too narcotic and requires more reduction than the other candidates.
10. **Any Heller or Gurlitt number selected solely because it is easy** — reject. These collections contain useful material, but the exact movement must pass a harmony/cadence/texture audition; otherwise they reproduce the “basic exercise” problem.

## Recommended verification and implementation gate

Before implementation, inspect only the top seven in historical scans and capture this small checklist:

1. Record the exact scan filename, editor/publisher, publication year, IMSLP file number, and regional copyright tag.
2. Confirm title, catalogue, key, meter, printed tempo, upbeat, and bar numbering.
3. Play the proposed span at 72–92 BPM-equivalent and confirm it closes without borrowing a later cadence.
4. Roman-numeral-check the last two bars. Prefer a root-position authentic tonic arrival with the melody on scale degree 1; avoid stopping on V, deceptive cadences, or first-inversion tonic unless a designed tail resolves it.
5. Compare any machine-readable source note-for-note and rhythm-for-rhythm against the scan. Preserve the source/license header in the repository.
6. Test three reductions: **piano only**, **melody pings + soft piano**, and **melody strings + arpeggiated piano**. Reject pieces that need thick Romantic pedaling/doubling to remain convincing.
